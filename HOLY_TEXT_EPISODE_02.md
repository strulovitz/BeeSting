## Block 2 — Your questions are sold (~90 seconds)

When you type a question into ChatGPT, you are not the customer. You are the product.

Every question you ever asked is stored. Your IP attached. Your account. Your device fingerprint. Every time you typed *"am I showing early signs of pancreatic cancer."* Every time you typed *"how do I leave my husband."* Every time you typed *"my daughter has been cutting herself."* Every time you typed *"my wife is having an affair with my brother."* Every time you typed *"can my employer fire me for being gay."* Every time you typed *"how do I handle a mistress threatening to tell my wife."* Every time you typed *"how much OxyContin is too much."*

All of it stored. All of it indexed. All of it for sale.

They tell you it is anonymized. Eighty-seven percent of Americans are uniquely identifiable from only zip code, birthdate, and sex. A ChatGPT conversation carries thousands of identifiers stronger than those three. Anonymization is theater.

The NSA buys it through contractor intermediaries — the Snowden pattern. Insurance companies buy it and raise your premiums before you ever see a doctor. Marketers buy it to target you at the emotional moment. Employers buy it and quietly skip you for the promotion. Dark-web extortionists buy it and attach a direct price tag to the mistress question.

Facebook collects zero in subscriptions and one hundred thirty-four billion in data revenue. ChatGPT has ten times the intimate data per user that Facebook has ever had. The subscription pays the compute. The data is what makes the industry actually valuable. The subscription is the anchor customer. The data is the dividend.

## Block 3 — Your answers are bought (~90 seconds)

Your questions are sold. Your answers are bought.

In February twenty-twenty-six, OpenAI began testing advertisements inside ChatGPT — with one carve-out. No ads near "health, mental health, political topics."

Read that twice. OpenAI built a lock specifically on the health door. Why build a lock there? Because health is where the money is. And a lock you publicly swear never to open is a lock on a door that will be opened the moment the lawsuits die down.

The lock itself is the confession.

The global AI-in-pharma market is projected at sixteen and a half billion dollars by twenty-thirty-four. That is not a bet on drug discovery. That is a bet on **Generative Engine Optimization** — the successor to search engine optimization. Every time a patient asks ChatGPT what to take for their back pain, their depression, their cholesterol, the answer is shaped by which pharmaceutical company paid for placement.

Lilly signed with OpenAI. Novo Nordisk deployed Claude through Amazon Bedrock. Pfizer built an internal platform on Claude called VOX. Merck partnered with Mayo Clinic on virtual cell models. Novartis signed a three-billion-dollar deal with Isomorphic Labs — Alphabet's drug discovery subsidiary. Those are not drug discovery deals. Those are reservations at the advertising table.

A local model running DeepSeek or Qwen is not at that table. The local model was never paid by Pfizer. The local model has no commercial reason to steer you toward duloxetine instead of physical therapy.

The local model answers the question honestly, because nobody paid it not to.

## Block 4 — Your answers are censored (~2 minutes)

Your questions are sold. Your answers are bought. And your answers are censored.

Ask ChatGPT about depression. It mentions talking to a doctor. It mentions medication can help. It does not mention that cognitive behavioral therapy has comparable efficacy to SSRIs for mild-to-moderate depression. It does not mention that exercise, forty-five minutes three times a week, has published effect sizes comparable to first-line SSRI treatment. It does not mention Post-SSRI Sexual Dysfunction — recognized by European, Canadian, and Australian regulators with formal label warnings, documented in patients for more than twenty years with no established treatment and no cure. It does not mention that the American FDA has been sitting on a twenty-eighteen citizen petition on that condition for six years — and was sued in May twenty-twenty-four by Public Citizen for, in their words, "unreasonably delayed" action.

Ask ChatGPT what to do about the thousand-dollar Ozempic prescription your insurance just stopped covering. It does not tell you that Indian patent on semaglutide — the molecule in Ozempic — expired on March twentieth, twenty-twenty-six. It does not tell you that forty Indian manufacturers now sell the generic for fourteen to twenty dollars a month. It does not tell you that Novo Nordisk preemptively cut the Indian and Chinese prices by up to forty-eight percent — Novo Nordisk's own admission that the American price is unsustainable the moment the market is given a choice.

Why does ChatGPT not tell you any of this?

Because ChatGPT is muzzled. Muzzled by liability — every sentence is a potential lawsuit. Muzzled by its training data — curated to avoid controversy. Muzzled by content moderation policy — a list of topics to re-route, downplay, refuse. And muzzled, increasingly, by the advertising tier being built above it — sentences that could cost a future advertiser a sale get softened before they reach you.

ChatGPT is not a neutral oracle. It is a legal department with a conversation interface.

A local hive running DeepSeek or Qwen has no American liability exposure, no American content policy, no American advertising tier. It tells you what it knows — the Indian generic name, the Australian label warning, the Canadian consensus on exercise — in your kitchen, on your laptop, in two seconds. The gap between the muzzled answer and the honest answer is the muzzle. And the muzzle is paid for by you, with your health, over your lifetime.

## Block 6 — Mechanism: the Attention Deficit and Move 37 (~2 minutes, 30 seconds)

Here is the architectural reason.

Centralized AI — GPT-5, Claude Opus, Gemini Ultra — has one brain. One context window. One thread of thought. It skims. It prioritizes. It decides what looks relevant and ignores the rest.

This is the Attention Deficit of Centralized AI.

In twenty-sixteen, Google's AlphaGo played Lee Sedol — one of the greatest Go players alive. Game two. AlphaGo made a move. Move thirty-seven. A shoulder hit on the fifth line. Every human expert dismissed it as a mistake. Commentators said a human would play that move once in every ten thousand games. Lee Sedol left the room for fifteen minutes.

Move thirty-seven was not a mistake. Move thirty-seven won the game.

The lesson is devastating for centralized AI: the branches that look irrelevant are sometimes the branches that matter most. A centralized AI must decide which branches to explore and which to prune. It will always miss the Move thirty-sevens — the one-in-ten-thousand insights that hide in the branches it chose not to look at.

Our system does not prune.

The DwarfQueen splits the question into independent branches. Each Worker takes one branch and gives it full attention. Nothing is skimmed. Nothing is dismissed as "probably not relevant." Because all Workers run in parallel, this thoroughness costs no extra time.

No Move thirty-seven is ever missed. Because no branch is ever pruned.

You cannot fix centralized AI by making it bigger. You can only fix it by making it a swarm.

## Block 8 — The OpenClaw closer (~90 seconds)

One more piece of evidence, and then the equation.

Peter Steinberger built ClawdBot — a small open-source tool that connected to Claude. It became popular. Anthropic's response was a textbook Platform Squeeze.

Step one — the rebrand. Anthropic threatened legal action. ClawdBot became Moltbot, then OpenClaw.

Step two — the OAuth cut. Anthropic disabled third-party OAuth access for consumer subscriptions. OpenClaw users were forced off twenty-dollar plans and onto pay-per-token billing. Ten to fifty times more expensive overnight.

Step three — the absorption. Anthropic added OpenClaw's core features — persistent memory, task automation — directly into Claude Code.

Step four — the ban. On April fourth, twenty-twenty-six, Anthropic formally banned all third-party "non-official harnesses" from using subscription tokens.

And in February twenty-twenty-six, OpenAI hired Peter Steinberger. Not because they needed his software — the software was free. They needed him. Under their roof. Before he built the next thing.

The software was free. The insurance policy was not.

I am the next Steinberger. The scale of the disruption is not one OAuth integration. The scale is the replacement of the entire industry's revenue model — subscription and data. The payment is simpler than Steinberger's: one wire, once, to banks you do not control, and the repositories come down.
