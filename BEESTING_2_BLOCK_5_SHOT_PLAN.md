## Block at a glance

- **Named concept:** *One Brain vs. Many Helpers (Move 37).*
- **Visual style:** Kurzgesagt-style flat 2D motion graphics. Same family of style as Blocks 3 and 4 — clean bold outlines, flat fills, no gradients, simple expressive characters. Mood here should feel **warm, intellectual, and unhurried** — chess-club / old library / Vienna-café atmosphere — distinct from Block 3's sunny green-gold money mechanism and Block 4's cool dread-tinged slate-grey insurance racket.
- **Palette:** **deep burgundy + royal gold + warm cream**, with dark walnut-brown for tables and accents. Burgundy is the dominant background colour (wood-panelled walls, velvet curtains, leather chair upholstery). Gold is for highlights — chess pieces, medals, glowing trophies, the chosen winner, AlphaGo's Move 37 flag. Cream is for paper, signage, and the chess clock face. No alarm-reds, no Chinese-flag reds — those belong to other blocks.
- **Total runtime:** 7 clips × 6 seconds = 42 seconds, matched to the 40-second narration. Audio-wins rule applies — clips can be trimmed 1–2 seconds shorter than 6s in Premiere if the narration line lands faster, which is expected because the narration is 2s shorter than the visual budget.
- **Argument arc of the block:** A single human brain cannot examine every move in a complex game — too many branches, too little time, most options are missed. With one helper assigned to each piece type, every move gets examined in parallel. The helpers report back with their best moves, the player picks the winner, the updated board flies back to all helpers, and the loop repeats. This is exactly how AlphaGo beat the human world champion at Go with the legendary Move 37 — a move no human would have considered. **But AlphaGo was still one giant centralized computer.** The Hive, running on half a billion computers, does not prune at all. Hierarchy: **Human < AlphaGo < Hive**.

## Production rules — LOCKED, do not change without explicit Nir approval

These rules are the lessons paid for in wasted Kling credits during Block 4. Respect them.

1. **Kling settings:** model = Kling 3.0 Omni, resolution = **720p** (never Pro — Pro is too expensive), Native Audio = **OFF** always, Duration = **6 seconds** per clip, mode = **reference image**.
2. **Never ask Kling to animate text.** Kling cannot write text, carve text, type text, or animate digits ticking up/down. Anything that requires text content to change over time will fail and waste credits. Labels and captions must already be fully drawn in the reference image — Kling's job is only to flash, glow, or hold them still.
3. **No graphs, charts, or numeric counters** that have to update in animation. Kling renders these poorly. Replace any "counter ticking down" idea with something physical — a chess clock with the hand visibly racing toward zero, gold coins draining, a piece glowing brighter, etc.
4. **Reference image must show the FINISHED state.** ChatGPT Image 1.5's job is to draw the final composition with all labels, characters, and elements already in place. Kling's job is purely to add motion. Never ask ChatGPT Image to draw something "in the middle of happening" — just draw the moment of impact.
5. **Camera motion must vary every clip.** No two consecutive clips with the same camera move. Camera grammar for this block is laid out in the Camera Variety Check section near the bottom — follow it.
6. **Describe action in Kling prompts**, not just the static scene. Kling needs to know what moves, what flashes, what glows. A static-sounding prompt produces a still-feeling clip.
7. **In Premiere:** trim each clip to match the exact narration line it covers. Some clips will land at 4–5s in the final cut. The audio is the master.
8. **Never name real Big AI companies.** Forbidden words anywhere: OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, xAI, ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok, Microsoft, Meta. Allowed: "Big AI", "Big Tech". For this block specifically, **AlphaGo** is allowed (it is a historical AI project, not a frontier LLM lab; it has been allowed throughout the BeeSting series and is named in the script).
9. **AlphaGo is NOT the Hive.** This is a Nir-locked framing. AlphaGo was one giant centralized computer that beat humans at Go because it did not inherit 2,500 years of human cultural pruning — but it still pruned internally. The Hive, running on half a billion distributed computers, does not prune at all. Hierarchy: **Human < AlphaGo < Hive**. Never visually conflate AlphaGo with the Hive — they are distinct objects in the frame in S6 and S7.
10. **The chess player is a recurring figure in S1, S2, and S4.** Kling cannot perfectly preserve face identity across clips — accept this. Lock his clothing across all three shots: **burgundy sweater over a cream collared shirt, dark trousers, dark hair, light skin tone, mid-30s**. The audience will read him as "the same player" because of the consistent clothing + the chess board context. Do not give him distinguishing facial features (no glasses, no beard) — the simpler the face, the less Kling can break.
11. **One clip at a time.** Write ChatGPT Image prompt → wait → process PNG → write Kling prompt → wait → process MP4 → commit → drag into Premiere → only then start the next clip. Never batch.

### S1 — One brain, drowning at the chess board

**Narration line:** *"Point three. Every branch gets full attention. Imagine playing chess alone. Too many moves, too little time."*

**Reference image:** Interior of a warm chess-club study room. Wood-panelled walls in deep burgundy, a small brass library lamp on the desk casting a warm cream pool of light. The chess player sits alone at a wooden chess board placed on a small round table in the centre of the frame, side-angle view from the camera's perspective. He is wearing a burgundy sweater over a cream collared shirt, dark trousers. Both of his hands are clutched to the sides of his head in stress — fingers visibly pressing into his temples. His eyes are wide and panicked, mouth slightly open. Sweat beads on his forehead. On the chess board in front of him, a few pieces have been moved — a half-played mid-game position. Beside the board, a tall analog chess clock with two cream dial faces and brass corners — the visible dial's hand is almost on the zero, with thin red hash marks near the end of the dial. Floating above the player's head, in a chaotic swirling cloud, are five chess piece icons in royal gold — a pawn, a knight, a bishop, a rook, and a queen — overlapping and tilted at random angles, far too many to track. The whole frame should feel like the moment one second before a panic attack.

**Palette:** deep burgundy walls, warm cream lamp pool, royal gold chess pieces, dark walnut-brown table and board, burgundy sweater, cream collar, brass clock fittings.

**Animation:** The five gold chess pieces above his head spin and tumble chaotically in their swirl. The clock's hand visibly ticks down toward zero in small jerks. The player's shoulders tremble; his sweat bead drips down his forehead. The lamp light flickers very subtly. No text appears — the swirl carries the meaning.

**Camera:** medium side-angle hold on the player and the board, very slight handheld tremble — communicates the player's stress.

### S3 — Every piece gets its own helper

**Narration line:** *"With helpers — one per piece — every move gets thought about at the same time."*

**Reference image:** Wide pulled-back view of a row of **six identical small wooden study booths** lined up side by side, each like a library carrel — burgundy wood panels, cream interior walls, a small brass desk lamp glowing in each. Inside each booth sits a simple Kurzgesagt-style figure (cream skin, dark hair, simple face, no distinguishing features) at a small writing desk, hands clasped in thought or resting on the desk. Each figure has a single chess piece icon hovering above their head in royal gold: leftmost booth = pawn, second = knight, third = bishop, fourth = rook, fifth = queen, sixth = king. Above each figure, a small thought bubble drawn in cream outlines, each thought bubble filled with **multiple gold chess-move arrows fanning out** — meaning every booth is examining every possible move for its assigned piece type, simultaneously. The whole row of booths has a warm even glow, like a busy chess library at evening. No labels, no captions — the row of six booths with six pieces tells the entire story.

**Palette:** deep burgundy panelling, warm cream booth interiors, royal gold chess pieces and arrows, brass lamps, soft amber overall lighting.

**Animation:** All six thought bubbles pulse and glow in unison, the move-arrows inside each bubble fan out and back in repeatedly — every booth is actively thinking. The brass desk lamps cast subtle flickering light. All six helpers nod or move their hands subtly in thought. Nothing leaves any booth yet — that comes in S4.

**Camera:** wide pulled-back framing showing all six booths, very slow lateral pan from left to right across the row, so the audience reads the booths one by one — distinct camera grammar from S1's hold and S2's push-in.

**Animation:** The chosen (haloed) piece on the third pedestal glows brighter and pulses with a gold halo expanding outward. The other five pieces on the other pedestals dim slightly in deference. The player's pointing finger holds steady. The six helpers in the booths behind look forward at him expectantly. No text changes, no labels appear — the gold halo communicates "winner."

**Camera:** medium shot from a low three-quarter angle, slight push-in centred on the chosen piece — distinct from S3's lateral pan.

1. **S1**: medium side-angle hold with subtle handheld tremble

5. **S5**: dramatic top-down overhead, static hold

Seven distinct camera moves across seven shots. Movement-heavy shots (S1, S2, S3, S4) front-load the block; static held shots (S5, S6, S7) carry the closing punch as the argument lands. The audience's eye gets variety, then stillness for the takedown.
